## The Problem — Told as a Story

Jordan Williams did everything right.

Four years of late nights in the library. Internships that paid barely enough to cover gas. A capstone project that their professor said was "the best in the class." A GitHub profile with green squares. A resume polished by the university career center.

Graduation day was May 9, 2026.

Three months later, Jordan has sent out **87 applications**.

They've heard back from **6**.

Three were automated rejections. One was a "we've decided to move forward with other candidates" — generic, no feedback. Two were "we'll keep your resume on file." One actually led to a phone screen — a recruiter who sounded bored, asked questions that felt scripted, and promised to "circle back."

That was six weeks ago. No circle back.

## The Daily Reality

Jordan's current routine:

- **7:30 AM** — Wake up, check phone. No emails from recruiters. Open LinkedIn. Scroll through posts from former classmates announcing their new jobs. Close LinkedIn.
- **9:00 AM** — Open laptop. Scan Indeed, LinkedIn, Glassdoor, Otta. Apply to everything that mentions "junior," "associate," "entry-level," or "new grad." Most postings want 3-5 years of experience for roles called "Entry Level."
- **12:00 PM** — Lunch with mom. She asks, "Any calls?" Jordan says, "Still waiting." She means well.
- **1:00 PM** — More applications. Some ask for cover letters. Jordan has seven templates. They're starting to sound the same even to Jordan.
- **4:00 PM** — The anxiety hits. Jordan opens job descriptions for "AI Engineer" roles. Requirements list: Python, PyTorch, TensorFlow, Kubernetes, Docker, AWS, CI/CD, RAG pipelines, LLM fine-tuning, vector databases, MLOps. Jordan knows Python. They've heard of Docker. The gap between what Jordan knows and what the market wants feels like a canyon.
- **6:00 PM** — Dinner. Jordan's dad says, "Did you try that career center again?" Jordan nods and changes the subject.
- **9:00 PM** — Can't sleep. Jordan opens YouTube, searches "how to become an AI engineer." Watches a 45-minute video. The YouTuber says "it's easier than you think" and then lists 27 technologies Jordan has barely touched.
- **11:00 PM** — Still awake. Opens ChatGPT. Types "What skills do I need to get an AI engineering job?" ChatGPT gives a list. Jordan saves it. It's the same list. Always the same list.

## What Makes This Hurt

Jordan is not lazy. Jordan is not unprepared. Jordan is **invisible**.

The problem isn't that Jordan can't learn the skills. The problem is:

1. **No one will tell Jordan what's missing.** Recruiters don't give feedback. Rejection emails are form letters. The career center gave generic advice — "use action verbs," "quantify your impact" — but nothing specific to Jordan's actual profile.

2. **The goalposts keep moving.** Every job description is different. One wants AWS. Another wants GCP. This one wants RAG experience. That one wants fine-tuning. Jordan doesn't know which skills actually matter and which are "nice to haves" in disguise.

3. **The spray-and-pray cycle.** Without knowing what's specifically missing, Jordan applies to everything. The more rejections pile up, the more tempted Jordan is to apply even more broadly. It's a panic loop.

4. **The ego tax.** Every rejection chips away at something. Jordan was a good student. Jordan's professors said they had potential. But the market doesn't care about potential — it cares about what you can do on day one. And Jordan doesn't know if they can do what's needed.

## The Hidden Truths Jordan Won't Admit

- **Resume anxiety:** Jordan knows their resume is weak but doesn't know *how* it's weak. They've never gotten specific feedback from someone who actually hires engineers.
- **Portfolio uncertainty:** Jordan has 3 projects on GitHub. Are they impressive for an entry-level candidate? Or do they look like cookie-cutter tutorials? No one will say.
- **Direction confusion:** "AI Engineer" sounded right. But Jordan also sees "Data Engineer," "ML Engineer," "Software Engineer (AI)," "Applied Scientist." The titles blur together. Is Jordan aiming for the right target?
- **Interview fear:** Jordan hasn't had a real technical interview since an internship two years ago. If one came tomorrow, Jordan isn't sure they'd pass.

It's a Tuesday night. Jordan is doom-scrolling Reddit — r/cscareerquestions — reading yet another post titled *"300 applications, 0 offers. What am I doing wrong?"* The comments section is a graveyard of similar stories. Someone replies: *"You need someone to actually look at your resume and be honest with you. Chatbots won't do it."*

That's the part that gets them. Not generic advice. Not "build more projects." **A comparison.** Real market data. Specific, honest feedback.

## What Success Looks Like for Jordan

### Short-term (first session)
- Jordan describes their background and career goal
- The agent builds a complete profile: skills, projects, resume, GitHub
- The agent researches current market requirements for entry-level AI engineers
- Jordan gets a specific, prioritized gap analysis — not "you need more skills" but *"for AI Engineer at entry-level, you're strongest in Python (8/10) and JavaScript (7/10). You're missing Docker and cloud basics (AWS/GCP) — these appear in 73% of entry-level postings. Here's a 4-week plan to close this gap."*
- Jordan's resume gets specific feedback — not "use action verbs" but *"Your first project at XYZ Corp should lead with the impact, not the task. Instead of 'Built a dashboard,' write 'Reduced customer churn reporting time by 40% with a React dashboard serving 200+ daily users.'"*

### Medium-term (1-3 months)
- Jordan follows the learning roadmap, building the right skills in the right order
- Resume optimized with specific, recruiter-tested language
- GitHub and portfolio reviewed and improved
- Jordan starts applying with a targeted strategy, not spray-and-pray

### Long-term (3-6 months)
- Mock interviews for specific target roles
- Interview feedback: *"Your technical answer was correct, but your explanation structure could be clearer. Try: (1) clarify the problem, (2) propose a solution, (3) discuss tradeoffs, (4) handle edge cases."*
- 1 in 3 interviews converts to next round or offer
- **Jordan gets the job.**

## Design Implications from This Backstory

1. **Honesty over politeness.** Jordan has had enough generic encouragement. The agent must be specific, direct, and honest — even when the truth is uncomfortable.

2. **Comparisons not absolutes.** Don't say "your resume needs work." Say "compared to entry-level AI engineers who get interviews, your resume is missing quantifiable impacts in 3 of 5 projects."

3. **Actionable, not informative.** Every piece of feedback must come with a specific next step. "Your GitHub needs a README with architecture decisions" → "Here's a template. Your project X would benefit from explaining why you chose Postgres over MongoDB."

4. **Progress visibility.** Jordan needs to see change over time. "Your resume score went from 4/10 to 7/10. Your technical skill coverage went from 35% to 68%. You're ready to start applying."

5. **Celebrate the small wins.** When Jordan gets a callback, update the roadmap. When they pass a first round, adjust interview prep focus. The agent should feel like it's *on Jordan's team*, celebrating progress and adjusting strategy.
